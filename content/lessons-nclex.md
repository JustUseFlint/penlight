@lesson ST1 | How the NCLEX Thinks | Test-Taking | 6
The NCLEX is a computer-adaptive test (CAT). It adjusts difficulty to your answers and stops when it is statistically confident you pass or fail. It tests **safe, entry-level clinical judgment**, not memorization.
## Question types
- **Multiple choice** with one best answer.
- **Select all that apply (SATA):** treat each option as a true/false statement.
- Also: ordered response, fill-in-the-blank calculations, hot spot, chart/exhibit, matrix, and case studies (Next Generation NCLEX).
## Keys to reading a question
- Find the **question stem** and the key words: *first, priority, best, most important, immediate, further teaching needed.*
- Identify who is asking: you are the nurse, and you are safe, competent, and follow policy.
- Eliminate options that are unsafe, outside the nurse's scope, or do not address the problem.
> Must know: "Further teaching needed" and "which statement indicates a need for more instruction" ask you to pick the INCORRECT statement.

@lesson ST2 | ABCs, Maslow, and the Nursing Process | Test-Taking | 7
## Prioritization frameworks
- **ABCs:** Airway, Breathing, Circulation. Airway comes first, then breathing, then circulation.
- **Maslow's hierarchy:** physiological needs (oxygen, fluids, nutrition, elimination) before safety, love/belonging, esteem, and self-actualization.
- **Acute before chronic. Unstable before stable. Actual problems before potential problems.**
- **Safety:** if a problem threatens safety, address it first.
## Nursing process (ADPIE)
1. **Assessment** (collect data) comes first in most "what should the nurse do first?" questions.
2. **Diagnosis** (analyze data).
3. **Planning** (set goals and outcomes).
4. **Implementation** (carry out interventions).
5. **Evaluation** (did it work?).
> Must know: When a question asks for the first action, and assessment is an option and the client is not in immediate danger, assess before you intervene.

@lesson MC1 | Delegation and Supervision | Management of Care | 7
The RN is accountable for delegated tasks. Use the five rights of delegation: right task, circumstance, person, direction/communication, and supervision/evaluation.
## What the RN cannot delegate
Nursing process work that requires nursing judgment: **initial assessment, nursing diagnosis, care planning, patient teaching, evaluation,** and care of **unstable** clients.
## Who can do what
- **Unlicensed assistive personnel (UAP/NA):** activities of daily living, bathing, feeding stable clients, ambulating, turning, vital signs on stable clients, intake and output, specimen collection (such as a urine sample).
- **LPN/LVN:** stable clients with predictable outcomes; focused data collection, oral and many injectable meds, wound care, tracheostomy care, urinary catheters, reinforcing teaching. Not initial assessments, not unstable clients, and not IV push in most states.
- **RN:** assessment, teaching, IV pushes, blood transfusions, central lines, unstable clients, care planning.
> Must know: Assign the stable client to the least-trained person who can safely care for them, and keep the unstable or new-diagnosis client with the RN.

@lesson MC2 | Legal and Ethical Practice | Management of Care | 7
## Consent and rights
- **Informed consent** is obtained by the provider performing the procedure. The nurse **witnesses** the signature and makes sure the client understands and is not sedated or coerced.
- Clients have the right to refuse treatment. Respect the choice, provide information, and document.
- **Advance directives** (living will, healthcare proxy) state wishes if the client cannot decide.
## Confidentiality (HIPAA)
Share information only with those who need it for care. Do not discuss clients in public areas or look up records without a reason.
## Ethical principles
- **Autonomy** (self-determination), **beneficence** (do good), **nonmaleficence** (do no harm), **justice** (fairness), **fidelity** (keep promises), **veracity** (truthfulness).
## Errors and incidents
Care for the client first, notify the provider, then complete an incident report. The report is not referenced in the chart.
> Must know: A nurse who sees a coworker practicing unsafely follows the chain of command and reports; the nurse never ignores it.

@lesson MC3 | Prioritization and Triage | Management of Care | 7
## Which client first?
Use ABCs, then acute/unstable. A client with new chest pain, sudden shortness of breath, a new neurological deficit, or active hemorrhage is seen first.
## Triage categories (disaster)
- **Red (emergent):** life-threatening but treatable (airway obstruction, shock, severe bleeding).
- **Yellow (urgent):** serious but can wait (stable fractures).
- **Green (minor):** walking wounded.
- **Black (expectant):** unlikely to survive or deceased.
## Typical priority order
1. Airway/breathing problems.
2. Circulation problems (shock, uncontrolled bleeding).
3. Altered mental status or new neuro changes.
4. Acute pain and abnormal vital signs.
5. Stable chronic conditions and routine care.
> Must know: A client who can speak in full sentences has an airway. A client who is quiet and cyanotic is in trouble.

@lesson SI1 | Standard and Transmission-Based Precautions | Safety & Infection Control | 8
**Standard precautions** apply to ALL clients: hand hygiene, gloves with contact with body fluids, masks/eye protection for splashes, safe sharps handling.
## Transmission-based
- **Airborne:** negative-pressure room, **N95**. Think **TB, measles, varicella** (varicella also needs contact precautions).
- **Droplet:** surgical mask, private room; droplets travel a short distance (about 3 to 6 feet). Think **influenza, pertussis, mumps, rubella, meningitis, mycoplasma pneumonia**.
- **Contact:** gown and gloves. Think **MRSA, VRE, C. difficile, scabies, RSV, norovirus**.
- **C. difficile:** wash hands with **soap and water**, because alcohol does not kill spores.
- **Protective environment:** for severely immunosuppressed clients (neutropenia, transplant).
## PPE order
- **Put on:** gown, mask/respirator, goggles/face shield, gloves.
- **Take off:** gloves, goggles/face shield, gown, mask. Perform hand hygiene afterward.
> Must know: Airborne = N95 and negative pressure; droplet = surgical mask; contact = gown and gloves.

@lesson SI2 | Client Safety: Falls, Restraints, and Fire | Safety & Infection Control | 7
## Falls
- Risk factors: older age, confusion, sedatives, weakness, previous falls, urgency.
- Prevent: bed low and locked, call light within reach, non-skid footwear, regular toileting rounds, bed/chair alarms, adequate lighting.
## Restraints (last resort)
- Use the **least restrictive** option, only after alternatives fail.
- Require a **provider order** (time-limited) and ongoing reassessment. Check circulation, skin, and range of motion frequently, and offer toileting, fluids, and repositioning regularly.
- Never tie to the bed rail; use quick-release knots tied to the bed frame.
## Fire: RACE and PASS
- **R**escue, **A**larm, **C**ontain (close doors), **E**xtinguish/evacuate.
- Extinguisher: **P**ull, **A**im at the base, **S**queeze, **S**weep.
- Shut off oxygen at the zone valve as directed.
> Must know: Restraints are a last resort and require an order; a restrained client needs frequent monitoring.

@lesson SI3 | Sterile Technique and Equipment Safety | Safety & Infection Control | 6
## Surgical asepsis
- A sterile object touching a non-sterile object becomes contaminated.
- Keep sterile items **above waist level** and in view. Do not turn your back on a sterile field.
- The **outer 1 inch (2.5 cm)** border of a sterile field is considered contaminated.
- **Wet** sterile surfaces are contaminated (moisture wicks bacteria).
- Open sterile packages **away from you** first, then to the sides and toward you.
## Sharps and equipment
- Never recap needles. Place sharps directly into a puncture-resistant container.
- Needlestick: wash with soap and water, report immediately, follow post-exposure protocol.
- Check electrical equipment for frayed cords; use three-prong plugs.
> Must know: If you are unsure whether something is sterile, it is not. Discard it and start again.

@lesson HP1 | Growth and Development | Health Promotion & Maintenance | 8
## Erikson
Infant: trust vs. mistrust. Toddler: autonomy vs. shame/doubt. Preschool: initiative vs. guilt. School-age: industry vs. inferiority. Adolescent: identity vs. role confusion. Young adult: intimacy vs. isolation. Middle adult: generativity vs. stagnation. Older adult: integrity vs. despair.

Sensorimotor (birth to 2), preoperational (2 to 7), concrete operational (7 to 11), formal operational (12 and up).

- Social smile about 2 months. Rolls over about 4 to 5 months. Sits without support about 6 months. Walks about 12 months. Two-word phrases about 2 years.

- Infants: back to sleep, no loose bedding. Toddlers: poisoning, drowning, choking. School-age: helmets and seat belts.

@lesson HP2 | Maternal and Newborn Basics | Health Promotion & Maintenance | 8
## Pregnancy
- **Naegele's rule:** first day of last menstrual period − 3 months + 7 days.
- **Preeclampsia:** BP 140/90 or higher after 20 weeks with proteinuria or other severe features. Watch for headache, visual changes, epigastric pain, hyperreflexia.
- **Magnesium sulfate** prevents seizures. Check **deep tendon reflexes, respiratory rate (at least 12), and urine output (at least 30 mL/hr)**. The antidote is **calcium gluconate**.
## Postpartum
- Fundus should be firm, midline, at or below the umbilicus. **Boggy** fundus: massage, then check for bladder distention.
- Lochia: rubra (red) → serosa (pink/brown) → alba (white/yellow).
- **Rh-negative mothers** with Rh-positive babies receive Rh immune globulin (RhoGAM) at about 28 weeks and within 72 hours after birth.
## Newborn
- **Apgar** at 1 and 5 minutes (heart rate, respiratory effort, muscle tone, reflex irritability, color); 7 to 10 is reassuring.
- Keep the newborn warm, support breastfeeding, give vitamin K, and monitor for jaundice.
> Must know: A boggy fundus after delivery risks hemorrhage. Massage the fundus first.

@lesson HP3 | Screening, Immunization, and Prevention | Health Promotion & Maintenance | 6
## Levels of prevention
- **Primary:** prevent disease (immunizations, healthy diet, seat belts).
- **Secondary:** early detection (screening mammograms, blood pressure checks, Pap tests).
- **Tertiary:** limit complications and rehabilitate (cardiac rehabilitation, physical therapy).
## Immunizations
- Live vaccines (MMR, varicella) are generally avoided in pregnancy and in severely immunocompromised clients.
- Mild illness is not a reason to delay; moderate or severe acute illness may be.
- Contraindication for any vaccine: a severe allergic reaction to a prior dose or component.
## Teaching principles
- Assess readiness to learn, use plain language, and use **teach-back** to confirm understanding.
- Match teaching to the client's developmental level, culture, and health literacy.
> Must know: Teach-back (asking the client to explain or demonstrate) is the best way to confirm learning.

@lesson PS1 | Therapeutic Communication | Psychosocial Integrity | 6
## Helpful techniques
- **Open-ended questions** ("How are you feeling?"), **active listening**, **reflection**, **clarifying**, **silence**, and **summarizing**.
- Use the client's own words and focus on feelings.
## Blocks to avoid
- "Why" questions (sound accusing), **false reassurance** ("Everything will be fine"), **giving advice**, changing the subject, disagreeing, or judging.
## Special situations
- **Angry client:** stay calm, keep distance, set limits, and use a low voice.
- **Hallucinations:** do not argue; acknowledge feelings and reality ("I don't see that, but I know it feels real to you").
- **Suicide risk:** ask directly about thoughts and plans, never promise secrecy, and remove hazards.
> Must know: The best response to a client who expresses feelings is one that invites more sharing, not one that advises or reassures.

@lesson PS2 | Crisis, Coping, Grief, and Abuse | Psychosocial Integrity | 7
- **Crisis** is an event that overwhelms usual coping. Priorities: safety, support, short-term focus on the present problem.
- **Defense mechanisms:** denial (refusing to accept), projection (blaming others), repression (forgetting), regression (acting younger), displacement (shifting feelings to a safer target), rationalization (making excuses), sublimation (channeling into acceptable activity).
## Kubler-Ross stages of grief
Denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance. People move back and forth; there is no set order.
## Abuse
- Assess in private; use direct, nonjudgmental questions.
- Ensure safety, document objectively (use the client's own words), and follow mandatory reporting laws for child, elder, and dependent adult abuse.
> Must know: Never leave a client who is actively suicidal alone; maintain constant observation and a safe environment.

@lesson PS3 | Psychiatric Disorders and Substance Use | Psychosocial Integrity | 8
- **Depression:** SSRIs take 2 to 6 weeks for full effect; watch suicide risk early in treatment; **serotonin syndrome** (agitation, fever, diaphoresis, tremor).
- **MAOIs** require avoiding tyramine-rich foods (aged cheese, cured meats) because of hypertensive crisis.
- **Bipolar:** lithium therapeutic level **0.6 to 1.2 mEq/L**; keep fluid and sodium intake steady; early toxicity includes coarse tremor, nausea, diarrhea.
- **Antipsychotics:** extrapyramidal symptoms, tardive dyskinesia, **neuroleptic malignant syndrome** (high fever, rigidity, altered mental status).
## Alcohol withdrawal
- Begins 6 to 24 hours after the last drink; delirium tremens 48 to 96 hours. Signs: tremor, anxiety, sweating, tachycardia, hallucinations, seizures.
- Treatment: benzodiazepines (monitored with CIWA), thiamine, fluids, a calm low-stimulation room.
## Opioid overdose
Respiratory depression and pinpoint pupils. Naloxone reverses it; monitor because naloxone may wear off before the opioid.
> Must know: Lithium toxicity risk rises with dehydration and low sodium.

@lesson BC1 | Mobility and Skin Integrity | Basic Care & Comfort | 7
## Pressure injuries
- Stage 1: intact skin with non-blanchable redness. Stage 2: partial thickness (blister or shallow open). Stage 3: full-thickness skin loss, fat visible. Stage 4: exposed muscle, tendon, or bone. Unstageable: base covered by slough/eschar. Deep tissue injury: persistent deep red/purple area.
- Prevent: **reposition at least every 2 hours**, use pressure-relieving surfaces, keep skin clean and dry, manage moisture, and promote protein and fluids. Use the **Braden scale** to assess risk.
- Do not massage reddened bony prominences.
## Positioning
- **Semi-Fowler's (30 to 45 degrees)** for aspiration risk and feeding. **Trendelenburg** is rarely used. **Sims** for enemas. **Prone** for some back care.
## Mobility
- Use a **gait belt**; assist the client toward their stronger side when using a cane (hold the cane in the hand opposite the weaker leg).
> Must know: Stage 1 pressure injuries are non-blanchable. Blanching redness is not Stage 1.

@lesson BC2 | Nutrition and Elimination | Basic Care & Comfort | 7
## Nutrition
- **Dysphagia:** upright for meals, chin tuck, thickened liquids as ordered, and monitor for coughing.
- **Enteral feeding:** keep head of bed at least 30 degrees (30 to 45), check residual per policy, and flush the tube. Verify tube placement per policy (x-ray is the gold standard for initial placement).
## Diets
- Low sodium for heart failure and hypertension. Renal diet: limits potassium, phosphorus, and sodium and may restrict protein and fluids. Clear liquid diet: broth, gelatin, clear juice.
## Elimination
- **Constipation** prevention: fluids, fiber, activity.
- **Ostomy:** stoma should be pink/red and moist; dark or black stoma means poor blood supply and must be reported. Empty the pouch when one-third to one-half full.
- Urinary catheters: keep the bag below bladder level, do not kink tubing, and remove as soon as possible to lower the risk of infection (CAUTI).
> Must know: A dusky or black stoma is an emergency: notify the provider.

@lesson BC3 | Pain, Comfort, and End-of-Life Care | Basic Care & Comfort | 6
- Pain is **whatever the client says it is**. Assess intensity, location, quality, onset, and what helps.
- Use both **pharmacological** (analgesics) and **non-pharmacological** methods (positioning, heat or cold, relaxation, distraction).
- Give pain medicine before painful procedures and before pain becomes severe. Reassess about 30 minutes after IV or 60 minutes after oral medication.
- With opioids, monitor **respiratory rate and sedation**, and start a bowel regimen.
## End-of-life care
- Comfort is the priority: pain control, mouth care, positioning, family support, and respect for cultural and spiritual needs.
- Signs of approaching death: decreased intake, mottling of extremities, **Cheyne-Stokes respirations**, terminal secretions. Hearing is thought to persist, so speak kindly at the bedside.
- **Hospice** provides comfort-focused care when life expectancy is about 6 months or less.
> Must know: Do not withhold pain medication for fear of addiction in a client with severe pain or at end of life.

@lesson PH1 | Medication Administration and Safety | Pharmacological & Parenteral Therapies | 7
## Rights
Right patient, drug, dose, route, time, documentation, reason, and response. Use **two identifiers** (name and date of birth), never the room number.
## Safety essentials
- Check allergies before giving anything new. Verify the order is complete and clear.
- Hold and clarify a medication when the order seems wrong or the client's vital signs or lab values make it unsafe.
- **High-alert drugs** (insulin, heparin, opioids, concentrated potassium) require independent double-checks.
- Never give concentrated potassium chloride by IV push.
## Routes
- **Sublingual:** let it dissolve; do not swallow. **Enteric-coated or extended-release:** do not crush.
- **IM injection:** 90-degree angle; **subcutaneous:** 45 to 90 degrees; ventrogluteal is a preferred IM site for adults.
> Must know: If you give a wrong medication, assess the client first, then notify the provider, then complete an incident report.

@lesson PH2 | IV Therapy and Blood Transfusion | Pharmacological & Parenteral Therapies | 8
## IV complications
- **Infiltration:** swelling, cool pale skin, pain at site. Stop infusion and remove the IV.
- **Phlebitis:** redness, warmth, tenderness along the vein. Remove and apply warm compress per policy.
- **Fluid overload:** crackles, dyspnea, edema. Slow infusion, raise head of bed, notify provider.
## Fluids
- Isotonic (0.9% saline, lactated Ringer's) expand volume. Hypotonic (0.45% saline) move water into cells. Hypertonic (3% saline) draw water out of cells.
## Blood transfusion
- Two nurses verify the client, the blood product, and compatibility at the bedside. Use only **0.9% normal saline** with blood.
- Stay with the client for the first **15 minutes** and start slowly; check vital signs before, at 15 minutes, and per policy.
- **Reaction signs:** fever, chills, flank/back pain, dyspnea, hypotension, hives. Action: **stop the transfusion, keep the IV open with new tubing and saline, notify the provider,** and send the blood bag and tubing to the lab.
> Must know: Stop the transfusion first when a reaction is suspected. Do not flush the existing tubing.

@lesson PH3 | Key Drug Classes and Antidotes | Pharmacological & Parenteral Therapies | 8
- **ACE inhibitors (-pril):** dry cough, hyperkalemia, angioedema. **ARBs (-sartan):** similar but less cough.
- **Beta blockers (-olol):** check heart rate and blood pressure; may mask signs of hypoglycemia; use caution in asthma.
- **Loop diuretics (furosemide):** monitor potassium, hearing (ototoxicity with rapid IV push), and daily weight.
- **Statins (-statin):** report muscle pain; monitor liver enzymes.
- **Warfarin:** INR 2 to 3; vitamin K antidote. **Heparin:** aPTT; protamine sulfate antidote.
- **Digoxin:** check apical pulse; hold for HR under 60 in adults; toxicity worsened by hypokalemia.
## Antidotes
Acetaminophen: acetylcysteine. Opioids: naloxone. Benzodiazepines: flumazenil. Digoxin: digoxin immune fab. Heparin: protamine. Warfarin: vitamin K. Magnesium sulfate: calcium gluconate.
> Must know: Beta blockers and ACE inhibitors both need blood pressure and pulse checks before administration; hold and call if the client is hypotensive.

@lesson RR2 | Perioperative Care | Reduction of Risk Potential | 7
## Before surgery
- Informed consent is obtained by the surgeon, **before sedation**. The nurse verifies it is signed and witnessed.
- Keep NPO per orders; confirm allergies, remove jewelry and dentures, and verify the surgical site and the surgical checklist ("time-out").
## After surgery (priorities)
- **Airway** and breathing first, then vital signs, level of consciousness, pain, surgical site, and drains.
- Prevent complications: incentive spirometry, deep breathing, early ambulation, leg exercises, and fluids.
- **Wound dehiscence:** separation of the wound edges. **Evisceration:** organs protrude. Cover with a **sterile saline-soaked dressing**, place the client in low Fowler's with knees bent, and notify the surgeon immediately.
- **Malignant hyperthermia:** rapidly rising temperature, muscle rigidity, tachycardia; give dantrolene.
> Must know: Hypotension with a rapid, weak pulse after surgery may indicate hemorrhage: check the dressing and notify the provider.

@lesson RR3 | Complications and Monitoring | Reduction of Risk Potential | 8
- **Shock (hypovolemic):** tachycardia, hypotension, cool clammy skin, low urine output, anxiety. Give fluids/blood, oxygen, and keep warm.
- **DVT:** unilateral leg swelling, warmth, pain. Do not massage the leg; elevate and notify the provider.
- **Pulmonary embolism:** sudden shortness of breath, chest pain, tachycardia, anxiety. Give oxygen, raise head of bed, call the provider.
- **Aspiration:** keep HOB up, check swallowing, suction as needed.

- **Cardiac catheterization:** keep the affected extremity straight, monitor distal pulses and the insertion site.
- **Bronchoscopy:** keep NPO until the gag reflex returns.
- **Paracentesis:** the client should void first; monitor vital signs and drainage.
## Assessment pearls
- A **change in level of consciousness** is often the earliest sign of deterioration.
> Must know: In shock, hypotension is a LATE sign; tachycardia and anxiety come earlier.

@lesson PA1 | Acid-Base Balance and ABGs | Physiological Adaptation | 8
Normal ABG values: **pH 7.35 to 7.45, PaCO₂ 35 to 45, HCO₃ 22 to 26, PaO₂ 80 to 100.**
## Interpret in three steps
1. pH: acidosis (<7.35) or alkalosis (>7.45).
2. Which value matches the pH? **CO₂** is respiratory (acid when high). **HCO₃** is metabolic (base when high).
3. Is there compensation? If both are abnormal and the pH is trending toward normal, partially compensated.
## ROME
**R**espiratory **O**pposite: pH down with CO₂ up (acidosis), or pH up with CO₂ down.
**M**etabolic **E**qual: pH and HCO₃ move in the same direction.
## Examples
- Respiratory acidosis: COPD, opioid overdose, hypoventilation.
- Respiratory alkalosis: anxiety, hyperventilation, pain.
- Metabolic acidosis: DKA, diarrhea, renal failure (Kussmaul respirations).
- Metabolic alkalosis: vomiting, NG suction.
> Must know: pH 7.30, CO₂ 55, HCO₃ 24 is uncompensated respiratory acidosis.

@lesson PA2 | Cardiac and Respiratory Emergencies | Physiological Adaptation | 9
## Myocardial infarction
- Chest pain (may radiate to arm/jaw), diaphoresis, nausea, shortness of breath. Women and diabetics may have atypical symptoms.
- Priorities: rapid assessment, 12-lead ECG, aspirin (chewed) unless contraindicated, **oxygen if SpO₂ is low**, nitroglycerin as ordered, pain control, and prompt reperfusion.
## Heart failure
- **Left-sided:** pulmonary congestion (crackles, dyspnea, orthopnea, cough).
- **Right-sided:** peripheral edema, JVD, weight gain, hepatomegaly.
- Care: diuretics, sodium restriction, daily weights (**a gain of 2 to 3 lb in a day or 5 lb in a week should be reported**), upright position.
## COPD and asthma
- COPD: low-flow oxygen (1 to 2 L/min; target SpO₂ about 88 to 92%), pursed-lip breathing, avoid over-oxygenation.
- Asthma: quick-relief inhaler (albuterol); signs of severe attack include silent chest.
> Must know: In COPD, a high oxygen flow can reduce the drive to breathe: use the lowest flow that works.

@lesson PA3 | Endocrine and Neurological Emergencies | Physiological Adaptation | 9
## Diabetes
- **Hypoglycemia:** shaky, sweaty, confused. **Rule of 15:** give 15 g fast-acting carbohydrate, recheck in 15 minutes. If unconscious, give IV dextrose or IM glucagon.
- **DKA** (type 1): high glucose, ketones, Kussmaul respirations, fruity breath, dehydration. IV fluids, insulin, potassium replacement per labs.
- **HHS** (type 2): very high glucose, severe dehydration, no significant ketosis.
## Stroke
- **FAST:** Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, Time to call 911. Note the time of onset: thrombolytic therapy has a narrow time window.
- A CT scan is done first to rule out hemorrhage; anticoagulants/thrombolytics are not given for hemorrhagic stroke.
## Increased intracranial pressure
- Raise the head of the bed about 30 degrees, keep the head midline, avoid straining.
- Cushing's triad: **hypertension with widened pulse pressure, bradycardia, irregular respirations.**
## Seizure
Protect the head, turn to side, loosen restrictive clothing, time it, and never put anything in the mouth.
> Must know: Never restrain a client during a seizure or place objects in the mouth.
