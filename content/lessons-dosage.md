@lesson DM1 | Metric Units and Conversions | Dosage Calculation | 6
Dosage math begins with unit fluency.
## The metric ladder
- 1 kg = 1,000 g; 1 g = 1,000 mg; 1 mg = 1,000 mcg.
- 1 L = 1,000 mL.
- **Move the decimal** 3 places to convert between adjacent units: g → mg (×1,000, move right), mcg → mg (÷1,000, move left).
## Common household equivalents
- 1 tsp = 5 mL; 1 tbsp = 15 mL; 1 oz = 30 mL; 1 cup = 240 mL.
- 1 kg = 2.2 lb; 1 inch = 2.54 cm.
## Safe-practice rules
- Use a **leading zero** (0.5 mg, never .5 mg) and **no trailing zero** (5 mg, never 5.0 mg).
- Write units out in full ("units," not "U").
- Always **double-check** the answer for reasonableness.
> Must know: This is educational drill math. In practice, follow your facility's policies and verify high-alert doses with a second nurse.

@lesson DM2 | Oral Dosing with Dimensional Analysis | Dosage Calculation | 7
**Dimensional analysis** sets up a chain of conversion fractions so units cancel.
## Template
Desired dose ÷ available strength × quantity = amount to give
**Example:** The order is 500 mg. Tablets are 250 mg each.
500 mg × (1 tablet / 250 mg) = **2 tablets**.
**Liquid example:** The order is 150 mg. The label reads 100 mg per 5 mL.
150 mg × (5 mL / 100 mg) = **7.5 mL**.
## Steps
1. Write the ordered amount with its unit.
2. Multiply by the conversion fraction(s) so unwanted units cancel.
3. Make sure the final unit is what you will give (tablets, mL).
4. Check reasonableness: is the answer close to what you'd expect?
> Must know: If units don't cancel to your desired unit, the setup is wrong.

@lesson DM3 | Weight-Based Dosing | Dosage Calculation | 7
Many drugs are ordered **per kilogram**. Always convert pounds to kilograms first (divide by 2.2).
## Steps
1. Convert weight to kg.
2. Multiply by the ordered dose per kg.
3. Divide by the concentration to get the volume.
**Example:** The order is 5 mg/kg. The client weighs 44 lb.
44 ÷ 2.2 = 20 kg. 20 kg × 5 mg/kg = **100 mg**.
## Daily doses divided
If the order is 30 mg/kg/day divided into 3 doses and the weight is 20 kg: 30 × 20 = 600 mg/day ÷ 3 = **200 mg per dose**.
> Must know: Use the weight in kilograms, rounded as your facility specifies. Check that the dose is within the safe range for the drug.

@lesson DM4 | IV Flow Rates | Dosage Calculation | 8
## Pump rate (mL/hr)
mL/hr = total volume (mL) ÷ time (hours).
**Example:** 1,000 mL over 8 hours → 125 mL/hr.
## Gravity drip rate (gtt/min)
gtt/min = (volume in mL × drop factor in gtt/mL) ÷ time in minutes.
**Example:** 500 mL over 4 hours (240 min) with a 15 gtt/mL set: (500 × 15) ÷ 240 = 31.25, so **31 gtt/min**.
- Macrodrip sets are usually 10, 15, or 20 gtt/mL. Microdrip sets are 60 gtt/mL, so gtt/min = mL/hr.
## Short infusions
A 100 mL piggyback over 30 minutes = 200 mL/hr on a pump.
> Must know: Round gtt/min to the nearest whole drop; round pump rates as your facility directs (commonly to the nearest tenth or whole number).

@lesson DM5 | Units-Based Drips and Reconstitution | Dosage Calculation | 7
## Heparin or insulin drips
Rate (mL/hr) = ordered units/hr ÷ concentration (units/mL).
**Example:** The order is 1,200 units/hr. The bag contains 25,000 units in 250 mL (100 units/mL). 1,200 ÷ 100 = **12 mL/hr**.
## Dose from an infusion
If a bag has 500 mg in 250 mL (2 mg/mL) running at 30 mL/hr: 30 × 2 = **60 mg/hr**.
## Reconstitution
A label may say "add 4.8 mL sterile water to make 250 mg/mL." After mixing, **use the concentration on the label**. Write the date, time, and your initials on the vial as the policy requires.
## Heparin/insulin safety
Both are high-alert drugs. Verify the concentration and the pump programming with a second nurse.
> Must know: Always confirm the concentration (units per mL) before calculating the pump rate.

@lesson DM6 | Pediatric Doses and Safe Dose Range | Dosage Calculation | 7
## Safe dose range
Compare the ordered dose with the reference range for the client's weight.
**Example:** The reference is 10 to 15 mg/kg/dose. The child weighs 18 kg.
Low: 18 × 10 = 180 mg. High: 18 × 15 = 270 mg. An ordered dose of 200 mg is **within the safe range**.
## Rounding
- Children's doses are often calculated to the **tenth** or hundredth for small volumes, and rounded **down** for safety if instructed.
- Volumes under 1 mL are usually measured with an oral syringe or a tuberculin syringe, and are rounded to the hundredth.
- Maximum safe volumes for injections are smaller in children.
## When something doesn't look right
If the calculated dose seems far too large or small, **stop, recalculate, and clarify with the prescriber and pharmacist**.
> Must know: A dose outside the safe range must be clarified before administration.
