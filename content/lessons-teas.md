@lesson R1 | Main Idea and Supporting Details | Reading | 6
The **main idea** is the single most important point the author makes about the topic. **Supporting details** are the facts, examples, and reasons that back it up.
## How to find the main idea
- Read the first and last sentences of the passage and of each paragraph. Authors often state the main idea there.
- Ask: "If I could keep only one sentence, which one would still tell me what this is about?"
- A good main idea is broad enough to cover every paragraph, but narrow enough to be specific to this passage.
## Traps on the TEAS
- **Too narrow:** an answer that is true but only covers one detail.
- **Too broad:** an answer that is true of the topic in general but not of this passage.
- **Not mentioned:** an answer that sounds reasonable but is not supported by the text.
> Must know: The correct answer is always supported by the passage. If you cannot point to the line that proves it, it is probably wrong.
## Quick method
1. Read the question first so you know what to look for.
2. Read the passage and underline the claim and the evidence.
3. Eliminate answers that are too narrow, too broad, or unsupported.

@lesson R2 | Inference, Purpose, and Tone | Reading | 6
An **inference** is a conclusion you reach from evidence in the text when the author does not say it directly. The TEAS asks for inferences that are strongly supported, not wild guesses.
## Making a safe inference
- Find the clues: specific words, facts, or behaviors in the passage.
- Choose the answer that must be true, or is the most probable, given those clues.
- Avoid answers that need outside knowledge or add new facts.
## Author's purpose
- **Inform:** facts, definitions, and neutral language.
- **Persuade:** opinion words, appeals to emotion or authority, a call to act.
- **Entertain:** story, humor, vivid description.
## Tone
Tone is the author's attitude. Look at word choice: "alarming" and "disastrous" signal concern; "steady" and "encouraging" signal optimism. Tone words to know: objective, critical, sympathetic, ironic, urgent, optimistic, skeptical.
> Must know: Inference answers are rooted in the passage. If it needs a fact that is not in the text, it is not an inference.

@lesson R3 | Text Structure, Fact vs. Opinion, and Text Features | Reading | 6
**Text structure** is how the author organizes ideas. Recognizing it helps you predict and locate information.
## Common structures and signal words
- **Chronological or sequence:** first, next, then, finally, afterward.
- **Compare and contrast:** however, similarly, on the other hand, both, unlike.
- **Cause and effect:** because, as a result, therefore, leads to.
- **Problem and solution:** the issue is, one solution, to address.
- **Description:** for example, such as, in addition.
## Fact vs. opinion
- A **fact** can be verified (a date, a measurement, a documented event).
- An **opinion** expresses a belief or judgment ("best," "should," "most likely," "I think").
## Text features
Headings, bold terms, captions, tables, and footnotes help you find information fast. Titles and headings often tell you the main idea of a section.
> Must know: Bias is a one-sided presentation. Look for loaded words, missing viewpoints, or claims without evidence.

@lesson R4 | Integrating Ideas: Graphs, Sources, and Arguments | Reading | 7
The TEAS asks you to combine information from different formats: text, charts, graphs, and more than one source.
## Reading data
- Read the title, axes labels, units, and key before you read the numbers.
- Match the question to the right row or line, then check units (percent versus count).
- Trends: look at direction (up, down, flat) and size of change.
## Evaluating an argument
- **Claim:** what the author wants you to believe.
- **Evidence:** facts, data, expert testimony.
- **Reasoning:** how the evidence connects to the claim.
- A strong argument has relevant, sufficient, accurate evidence. Be wary of anecdotes, emotional appeals that replace evidence, and overgeneralizing.
## Comparing sources
Two sources can agree, disagree, or add different details. Ask what each says, then what is common and what is different.
> Must know: Check the units and scale on every graph. Many wrong answers come from misreading a unit.

@lesson R5 | Vocabulary in Context and Reading Strategy | Reading | 5
You can often work out an unfamiliar word from the sentence around it.
## Context clues
- **Definition clue:** the word is defined right there ("A *prognosis*, or expected outcome...").
- **Synonym/antonym clue:** "unlike" or "but" signals an opposite; "also" or "similarly" signals a similar meaning.
- **Example clue:** examples after "such as" show the meaning.
- **Inference clue:** the overall meaning of the sentence points to the word.
## Word parts
Prefixes, roots, and suffixes unlock medical and general vocabulary: *hypo-* (under), *hyper-* (over), *brady-* (slow), *tachy-* (fast), *-itis* (inflammation), *-emia* (blood condition), *-ectomy* (removal).
## Test strategy
- Do not spend more than about a minute on one reading item.
- Skim the questions first; then read actively.
- Eliminate two answers, then choose the best of the remaining two.
> Must know: Try replacing the word with each answer choice. The one that keeps the sentence's meaning is correct.

@lesson M1 | Whole Numbers and Order of Operations | Math | 6
Work in this order: **P**arentheses, **E**xponents, **M**ultiplication and **D**ivision (left to right), **A**ddition and **S**ubtraction (left to right).
## Example
6 + 2 × (5 − 3)² = 6 + 2 × 2² = 6 + 2 × 4 = 6 + 8 = **14**
## Rules to remember
- Multiplication and division have equal rank; go left to right.
- Negative × negative = positive. Negative × positive = negative.
- Adding a negative is the same as subtracting.
## Number sense
- **Even** numbers end in 0, 2, 4, 6, 8. **Prime** numbers have exactly two factors (2, 3, 5, 7, 11...).
- **Estimating** helps you catch calculator or arithmetic mistakes: round, calculate, compare.
> Must know: Always finish what is inside parentheses first. Use estimation to check that an answer is reasonable.

@lesson M2 | Fractions and Decimals | Math | 7
## Fractions
- **Add/subtract:** find a common denominator, then add the numerators. 1/4 + 1/6 = 3/12 + 2/12 = 5/12.
- **Multiply:** multiply across. 2/3 × 3/5 = 6/15 = 2/5.
- **Divide:** multiply by the reciprocal. 2/3 ÷ 4/5 = 2/3 × 5/4 = 10/12 = 5/6.
- **Mixed numbers:** convert to improper fractions first. 1 1/2 = 3/2.
## Decimals
- Line up decimal points to add or subtract.
- To multiply, ignore the decimals, multiply, then place the decimal so the answer has as many decimal places as both factors combined.
- To divide by a decimal, move the decimal point in both numbers the same number of places until the divisor is a whole number.
## Converting
- Fraction to decimal: divide top by bottom (3/8 = 0.375).
- Decimal to fraction: 0.6 = 6/10 = 3/5.
> Must know: Compare fractions by converting to decimals or using a common denominator. A larger denominator means smaller pieces.

@lesson M3 | Percents and Percent Change | Math | 6
**Percent** means "per hundred."
## Core formulas
- Part = Percent × Whole. 20% of 60 = 0.20 × 60 = **12**.
- Percent = Part ÷ Whole × 100. 15 out of 60 = 25%.
- Whole = Part ÷ Percent. If 12 is 20%, whole = 12 ÷ 0.20 = 60.
## Percent change
Percent change = (New − Old) ÷ Old × 100. From 50 to 65: (65 − 50) ÷ 50 = 0.30 = **30% increase**.
## Discounts and tax
- Sale price = Original × (1 − discount). 25% off $80 → 80 × 0.75 = $60.
- With tax: multiply by (1 + tax rate).
> Must know: Percent change is always divided by the ORIGINAL value, not the new one.

@lesson M4 | Ratios, Proportions, and Rates | Math | 6
A **ratio** compares two quantities (3:2 or 3/2). A **proportion** says two ratios are equal. Solve by cross-multiplying.
## Example
If 4 tablets weigh 10 grams, how many grams do 10 tablets weigh?
4/10 = 10/x → 4x = 100 → x = **25 grams**.
## Rates and unit rates
A **rate** compares different units (miles per hour). A **unit rate** has 1 in the denominator: $12 for 4 items = $3 per item.
## Tips
- Keep the same units in the same position on both sides.
- Always check that your answer makes sense: more items should cost more.
> Must know: Set up the proportion so matching units line up (tablets over grams = tablets over grams).

@lesson M5 | Algebra: Equations and Word Problems | Math | 7
Solve for the unknown by doing the same operation to both sides.
## Steps
1. Simplify each side (distribute, combine like terms).
2. Move variable terms to one side, numbers to the other.
3. Divide by the coefficient.
Example: 3x + 5 = 20 → 3x = 15 → x = **5**.
## Inequalities
Solve the same way, but **reverse the sign when you multiply or divide by a negative**. −2x > 6 → x < −3.
## Word problems
- Define the variable ("Let x = number of hours").
- Translate: "is" means =, "of" means ×, "more than" means +, "less than" means − (in reverse order).
- Solve, then check units.
## Linear equations
y = mx + b: m is the slope (rate of change), b is the y-intercept (starting value).
> Must know: Flip the inequality sign when multiplying or dividing both sides by a negative number.

@lesson M6 | Measurement and Unit Conversions | Math | 7
The TEAS uses both metric and US customary units.
## Metric prefixes
kilo (1000), hecto, deka, **base**, deci (1/10), centi (1/100), milli (1/1000). Moving to a smaller unit means multiplying.
- 1 kg = 1000 g; 1 g = 1000 mg; 1 mg = 1000 mcg.
- 1 L = 1000 mL; 1 m = 100 cm = 1000 mm.
## Common equivalents
- 1 inch = 2.54 cm; 1 kg ≈ 2.2 lb; 1 lb = 16 oz.
- 1 tsp ≈ 5 mL; 1 tbsp = 3 tsp ≈ 15 mL; 1 cup = 8 fl oz; 1 pint = 2 cups; 1 quart = 2 pints; 1 gallon = 4 quarts.
## Dimensional analysis
Multiply by conversion fractions so units cancel. 150 lb × (1 kg / 2.2 lb) ≈ 68.2 kg.
> Must know: Write units in every step. If the units don't cancel to what you want, the setup is wrong.

@lesson M7 | Geometry, Statistics, and Data | Math | 7
## Geometry
- Area of a rectangle = length × width; triangle = ½ × base × height; circle = πr²; circumference = 2πr.
- Perimeter is the distance around. Volume of a rectangular prism = l × w × h.
- Angles in a triangle sum to 180°.
## Statistics
- **Mean:** sum ÷ count. **Median:** middle value after ordering. **Mode:** most frequent. **Range:** highest − lowest.
- Example: 4, 7, 7, 9, 13 → mean 8, median 7, mode 7, range 9.
## Reading data
- A bar graph compares categories; a line graph shows change over time; a pie chart shows parts of a whole.
- Probability = favorable outcomes ÷ total outcomes.
> Must know: Order the data before finding the median. With an even count, the median is the mean of the two middle values.

@lesson S1 | Cardiovascular and Respiratory Systems | Science | 8
## Heart and circulation
- The heart has four chambers: right atrium, right ventricle, left atrium, left ventricle.
- **Right side** pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs. **Left side** pumps oxygenated blood to the body.
- Blood flow: body → vena cava → right atrium → tricuspid valve → right ventricle → pulmonic valve → pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary veins → left atrium → mitral valve → left ventricle → aortic valve → aorta → body.
- **Arteries** carry blood away from the heart (thick, elastic walls). **Veins** carry blood toward the heart (valves prevent backflow). **Capillaries** are where exchange happens.
- Blood components: red blood cells (oxygen), white blood cells (defense), platelets (clotting), plasma.
## Respiration
- Air path: nose → pharynx → larynx → trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli.
- Gas exchange occurs in the **alveoli**: oxygen diffuses into blood; carbon dioxide diffuses out.
- The **diaphragm** contracts to inhale (chest volume increases, pressure falls) and relaxes to exhale.
> Must know: Pulmonary arteries carry deoxygenated blood; pulmonary veins carry oxygenated blood.

@lesson S2 | Nervous and Endocrine Systems | Science | 8
## Nervous system
- **Central nervous system (CNS):** brain and spinal cord. **Peripheral (PNS):** all other nerves.
- A **neuron** has dendrites (receive), a cell body, and an axon (sends); the myelin sheath speeds conduction. Neurons communicate across synapses using neurotransmitters.
- Brain: cerebrum (thought, voluntary movement), cerebellum (balance, coordination), brainstem (breathing, heart rate), hypothalamus (temperature, hunger, hormone control).
- **Sympathetic** = fight or flight (heart rate up). **Parasympathetic** = rest and digest (heart rate down).
## Endocrine system
Glands release hormones into the blood.
- Pituitary: "master gland" (growth hormone, ADH, TSH).
- Thyroid: thyroxine regulates metabolism. Parathyroid: raises blood calcium.
- Adrenal: epinephrine and cortisol. Pancreas: **insulin** lowers blood glucose; **glucagon** raises it.
> Must know: Negative feedback keeps the body in homeostasis; the response reduces the original stimulus.

@lesson S3 | Digestive, Urinary, and Reproductive Systems | Science | 8
## Digestion
- Path: mouth → esophagus → stomach → small intestine → large intestine → rectum.
- Mechanical digestion (chewing) and chemical digestion (enzymes: amylase for starch, pepsin for protein, lipase for fat).
- The small intestine (duodenum, jejunum, ileum) absorbs most nutrients. The large intestine absorbs water.
- Liver makes bile; gallbladder stores it; pancreas releases digestive enzymes and bicarbonate.
## Urinary system
- Kidneys filter blood at the nephron, make urine, and balance fluid, electrolytes, and pH. Urine flows kidney → ureter → bladder → urethra.
## Reproductive
- Male: testes make sperm and testosterone. Female: ovaries make eggs, estrogen, progesterone.
- Fertilization occurs in the fallopian tube; implantation occurs in the uterus.
> Must know: Most nutrient absorption happens in the small intestine; most water absorption happens in the large intestine.

@lesson S4 | Musculoskeletal, Integumentary, and Immune Systems | Science | 8
## Skeletal and muscular
- Bones provide support, protection, movement, mineral storage, and blood cell production (marrow).
- **Tendons** connect muscle to bone; **ligaments** connect bone to bone.
- Three muscle types: skeletal (voluntary), smooth (involuntary, organs), cardiac (involuntary, heart).
## Skin
- Layers: epidermis (outer, waterproof barrier), dermis (nerves, blood vessels, glands), hypodermis (fat).
- Functions: protection, temperature regulation, sensation, vitamin D synthesis.
## Immune and lymphatic
- **Innate immunity** is immediate and non-specific (skin, mucus, phagocytes). **Adaptive immunity** is specific and has memory (B cells make antibodies; T cells attack infected cells).
- **Active immunity:** your body makes antibodies (infection or vaccine). **Passive immunity:** antibodies are received (breast milk, antibody injection).
> Must know: Vaccines create active immunity; antibodies from a mother's milk are passive immunity.

@lesson S5 | Cells and Biological Molecules | Science | 7
## Cell structures
- **Nucleus:** DNA, controls the cell. **Mitochondria:** make ATP (energy). **Ribosomes:** build proteins. **Endoplasmic reticulum:** protein and lipid synthesis. **Golgi apparatus:** packages and ships. **Lysosomes:** digest waste. **Cell membrane:** controls what enters and leaves.
- Plant cells also have a cell wall, chloroplasts, and a large central vacuole. Animal cells do not.
- **Prokaryotes** (bacteria) have no nucleus; **eukaryotes** do.
## Transport
- **Diffusion:** high to low concentration, no energy. **Osmosis:** diffusion of water. **Active transport:** low to high concentration, needs ATP.
## Macromolecules
- Carbohydrates (energy), lipids (storage, membranes), proteins (structure, enzymes), nucleic acids (DNA/RNA).
> Must know: Mitochondria are the "powerhouse"; ATP is the cell's usable energy.

@lesson S6 | Genetics and Microorganisms | Science | 8
## Genetics
- DNA is a double helix of bases A-T and C-G. RNA uses U instead of T.
- Genes code for proteins. Alleles are versions of a gene: **dominant** alleles mask **recessive** alleles.
- **Genotype** is the allele makeup (Bb); **phenotype** is the observable trait. **Homozygous** = two identical alleles; **heterozygous** = two different.
- Punnett square: Bb × Bb gives 25% BB, 50% Bb, 25% bb, so a 3:1 dominant-to-recessive phenotype ratio.
- **Mitosis** makes two identical body cells. **Meiosis** makes four sex cells with half the chromosomes.
## Microorganisms
- Bacteria (antibiotics work), viruses (not living cells; antivirals/vaccines), fungi, and parasites.
- Normal flora help protect us. Handwashing is the single best prevention of infection spread.
> Must know: Antibiotics treat bacterial infections, not viral ones.

@lesson S7 | Basic Chemistry: Atoms, Bonds, and Reactions | Science | 7
## Atoms
- Protons (+) and neutrons (0) are in the nucleus; electrons (−) orbit it. The atomic number is the number of protons. Mass number = protons + neutrons.
- **Isotopes** have the same protons but different neutrons.
## Bonds
- **Ionic:** electrons transferred (NaCl). **Covalent:** electrons shared (H2O). **Hydrogen bonds:** weak attractions between molecules (give water its properties).
## States of matter
Solid, liquid, gas; changes include melting, freezing, evaporation, condensation, sublimation.
## Reactions
- Physical changes alter form, not identity (ice melting). Chemical changes create new substances (rusting, digestion).
- Balanced equations have equal atoms of each element on both sides.
> Must know: Electrons carry negative charge. An atom that loses an electron becomes a positive ion.

@lesson S8 | Acids, Bases, and Solutions | Science | 6
- **pH scale:** 0 to 14. Below 7 is acidic; 7 is neutral; above 7 is basic (alkaline). Each whole number is a tenfold change in hydrogen ion concentration.
- Blood pH is tightly kept near 7.35 to 7.45 by buffers.
- Acids donate H⁺ (hydrogen ions); bases accept H⁺ or release OH⁻.
## Solutions
- A **solution** has a **solute** dissolved in a **solvent** (water is the "universal solvent").
- Concentration describes how much solute is in a given amount of solution.
- **Neutralization:** acid + base → water + salt.
## Properties of water
Cohesion, high specific heat (resists temperature change), and polarity make water vital for life.
> Must know: A pH of 3 is ten times more acidic than a pH of 4.

@lesson S9 | Scientific Reasoning | Science | 7
## The scientific method
Question → hypothesis (testable, falsifiable prediction) → experiment → data → analysis → conclusion.
## Experimental design
- **Independent variable:** what the experimenter changes. **Dependent variable:** what is measured. **Controlled variables:** kept the same.
- A **control group** gets no treatment, for comparison. Larger sample sizes and **repeated trials** increase reliability.
- **Correlation** does not prove **causation**.
## Reading results
- Look for trends in tables and graphs. The independent variable goes on the x-axis; the dependent on the y-axis.
- A conclusion must be supported by the data, not go beyond it.
> Must know: Only one variable should be changed at a time in a fair test.

@lesson E1 | Parts of Speech and Subject-Verb Agreement | English & Language | 7
## Parts of speech
- **Noun** (person, place, thing), **pronoun** (replaces a noun), **verb** (action/state), **adjective** (describes a noun), **adverb** (describes a verb, adjective, or adverb), **preposition** (relationship: in, on, with), **conjunction** (joins), **interjection**.
## Subject-verb agreement
- Singular subjects take singular verbs: "The nurse **checks**." Plural subjects take plural verbs: "The nurses **check**."
- Ignore words between the subject and verb: "The box of supplies **is** empty."
- Compound subjects joined by *and* are plural: "The doctor and the nurse **are** here."
- With *or/nor*, the verb agrees with the closer subject: "Neither the nurses nor the doctor **is** late."
- Indefinite pronouns *each, everyone, somebody, either, neither* are singular.
> Must know: Find the true subject first, then match the verb. Prepositional phrases never contain the subject.

@lesson E2 | Sentence Structure and Punctuation | English & Language | 8
## Sentence types
- **Simple:** one independent clause. **Compound:** two independent clauses joined by a comma + conjunction or a semicolon. **Complex:** an independent plus a dependent clause. **Compound-complex:** both.
- Avoid **fragments** (missing subject, verb, or complete thought), **run-ons**, and **comma splices** (two independent clauses joined by only a comma).
## Punctuation
- **Comma:** after introductory phrases; in lists; before FANBOYS (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so) joining independent clauses; around nonessential information.
- **Semicolon:** joins two related independent clauses; separates complex list items.
- **Colon:** introduces a list or explanation after a complete sentence.
- **Apostrophe:** possession (nurse's chart, nurses' lounge) and contractions (it's = it is). *Its* is possessive.
> Must know: A semicolon must have a complete sentence on both sides.

@lesson E3 | Spelling, Commonly Confused Words, and Vocabulary | English & Language | 6
## Commonly confused words
- **their** (possessive) / **there** (place) / **they're** (they are).
- **affect** (verb: to influence) / **effect** (noun: result).
- **accept** (receive) / **except** (excluding).
- **principal** (main; school head) / **principle** (rule).
- **then** (time) / **than** (comparison).
- **its** (possessive) / **it's** (it is).
- **lose** (misplace) / **loose** (not tight).
## Plurals and spelling
- Most nouns add -s; nouns ending in s, x, z, ch, sh add -es; consonant + y becomes -ies (study → studies).
- "I before E except after C" has exceptions (weird, science, neighbor).
## Vocabulary
Use context and word parts. Know **homophones** (sound alike), **homographs** (spelled alike, different meaning), **synonyms**, and **antonyms**.
> Must know: Affect is usually a verb; effect is usually a noun. "The drug will affect you; the effect is drowsiness."

@lesson E4 | Writing: Organization, Clarity, and Transitions | English & Language | 6
- A paragraph has a **topic sentence**, supporting sentences, and a concluding sentence.
- Keep related ideas together and remove details that do not belong.
## Transition words
- Addition: also, furthermore, in addition.
- Contrast: however, but, on the other hand.
- Cause/effect: therefore, as a result, because.
- Sequence: first, next, finally.
- Example: for instance, such as.
## Clarity and concision
- Prefer active voice: "The nurse gave the medication" rather than "The medication was given by the nurse."
- Cut wordiness: "due to the fact that" → "because."
- Use **parallel structure**: "She likes reading, writing, and studying" (not "to study").
## Audience and tone
Use formal, standard English in professional writing; avoid slang and contractions.
> Must know: Parallel structure means using the same grammatical form for items in a list.

@lesson E5 | Pronouns, Modifiers, Tense, and Capitalization | English & Language | 6
## Pronouns
- Pronouns must agree with their **antecedent** in number and person: "Each nurse brought **her or his** badge."
- Subject pronouns (I, he, she, we, they) act; object pronouns (me, him, her, us, them) receive: "Between you and **me**."
- **Who** = subject; **whom** = object.
## Modifiers
- A **dangling modifier** has no clear subject: ✗ "Walking down the hall, the lights went out." ✓ "Walking down the hall, I saw the lights go out."
- Place modifiers next to what they describe.
## Verb tense
- Keep tense consistent within a sentence or passage. Past participles go with *have/has/had* ("has written," not "has wrote").
## Capitalization
- Capitalize proper nouns, first words of sentences, titles before names ("Dr. Lee"), days and months. Do not capitalize seasons or general directions.
> Must know: "Who" replaces he/she/they (subject); "whom" replaces him/her/them (object).
