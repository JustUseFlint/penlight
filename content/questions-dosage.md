@tag DM1
Q: Which is the correct way to write a dose of one-half milligram?
- .5 mg
- 0.50 mg
+ 0.5 mg
- ½ mg
R: Use a leading zero before the decimal point and no trailing zeros, to avoid tenfold errors.
Q: Which abbreviation should be avoided on medication orders?
- mg
+ U (for units)
- mL
- g
R: "U" can be mistaken for a zero. Write "units."
Q: 1 tablespoon equals how many milliliters (using household equivalents)?
- 5 mL
+ 15 mL
- 30 mL
- 240 mL
R: 1 tbsp = 15 mL; 1 tsp = 5 mL.

Q: The order is 0.25 g. The tablets are 250 mg. How many tablets will the nurse give?
- 0.25 tablet
+ 1 tablet
- 2 tablets
- 4 tablets
R: 0.25 g = 250 mg, so 1 tablet.
Q: Before giving a calculated dose, the nurse should
- skip the double-check.
+ check whether the amount is reasonable and verify high-alert drugs with another nurse.
- round up.
- trust the first calculation.
R: Reasonableness checks and independent double-checks catch errors.
@tag DM3
Q: A child weighs 33 pounds. What is the weight in kilograms?
- 7.5 kg
+ 15 kg
- 72.6 kg
- 30 kg
R: 33 ÷ 2.2 = 15 kg.
Q: A prescriber orders 6 mg/kg/day divided into 3 equal doses for a client who weighs 20 kg. How much is each dose?
- 20 mg
+ 40 mg
- 120 mg
- 6 mg
R: 6 × 20 = 120 mg/day ÷ 3 = 40 mg per dose.

Q: A client is to receive 1,000 mL of IV fluid over 8 hours by pump. What rate in mL/hr?
- 100
+ 125
- 150
- 80
R: 1,000 ÷ 8 = 125 mL/hr.
Q: A 100 mL antibiotic is to infuse over 30 minutes on a pump. What rate in mL/hr?
- 100
- 150
+ 200
- 300
R: 100 mL ÷ 0.5 hr = 200 mL/hr.
Q: What is the formula for the gravity drip rate in drops per minute?
- Volume (mL) × time (min) ÷ drop factor
+ Volume (mL) × drop factor ÷ time (min)
- Drop factor ÷ volume × time
- Volume ÷ drop factor × time
R: gtt/min = volume × drop factor ÷ time in minutes.
@tag DM5
Q: A heparin infusion bag contains 25,000 units in 250 mL. The order is 1,000 units/hr. What rate in mL/hr?
- 5
+ 10
- 25
- 100
R: Concentration is 100 units/mL; 1,000 ÷ 100 = 10 mL/hr.

- 150 mg
+ 250 mg
- 350 mg
- 400 mg
R: Range = 200 to 300 mg. Only 250 mg is within it.
Q: A calculated dose is far larger than the usual dose for the client's age and weight. What should the nurse do?
- Give it as calculated
+ Stop, recalculate, and clarify with the prescriber or pharmacist
- Give half
- Ask another client
R: Unusual doses must be verified before administration.
