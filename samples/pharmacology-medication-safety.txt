Welcome to this lecture on pharmacology basics and medication safety. Medication administration is one of the most important and highest-risk responsibilities of a nurse, so we are going to build a solid foundation. We will cover pharmacokinetics, the key vocabulary you will see on every exam, the rights of medication administration, and the high-alert medications that need extra attention.

Let's start with pharmacokinetics. Pharmacokinetics is defined as the study of what the body does to a drug. It has four processes that you can remember in order: absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion. Absorption refers to the movement of a drug from the site of administration into the bloodstream. Distribution is the transport of the drug through the blood to the tissues and its site of action. Metabolism, also called biotransformation, is the chemical breakdown of the drug, and it happens mostly in the liver. Excretion is the removal of the drug and its metabolites from the body, and the kidneys are the main organs of excretion.

Remember that liver and kidney function matter a great deal. If a patient has liver disease, drugs may build up because they are not metabolized well. If a patient has kidney disease, drugs may build up because they are not excreted well. In both situations the nurse should expect that the provider may lower the dose and should watch closely for signs of toxicity. Older adults are at greater risk because liver and kidney function decline with age.

The first-pass effect refers to the metabolism of an oral drug by the liver before it reaches the general circulation. Because of the first-pass effect, some drugs have to be given in much higher doses by mouth than by injection. Drugs given by the sublingual route, such as nitroglycerin, bypass the first-pass effect and are absorbed quickly through the mucous membranes under the tongue.

Pharmacodynamics is the study of what a drug does to the body. It describes the effect of the drug and how it works. An agonist is a drug that binds to a receptor and produces an effect. An antagonist is a drug that binds to a receptor and blocks the effect. Naloxone is a good example of an antagonist because it blocks opioid receptors and reverses an opioid overdose.

Now let's talk about the vocabulary you must know. The half-life of a drug is defined as the time it takes for the amount of drug in the body to decrease by half. Drugs with a short half-life need to be given more often. The onset of action is the time it takes for a drug to begin working. The peak is the time at which the drug reaches its highest concentration or effect. The duration is how long the drug keeps working. The therapeutic range is the blood level of a drug that provides the desired effect without causing toxicity. A trough level is the lowest concentration of a drug in the blood, and it is drawn just before the next dose is given. A peak level is drawn at the highest concentration, usually shortly after the drug has been given. Toxicity occurs when the drug level rises above the therapeutic range.

A side effect is a predictable, often mild, secondary effect of a drug. An adverse effect is an undesired and potentially harmful effect. An allergic reaction is an immune response to a drug. The most severe form is anaphylaxis, which is a life-threatening reaction with airway swelling, wheezing, hypotension, and hives. If a patient develops signs of anaphylaxis, stop the medication, call for help, maintain the airway, and give epinephrine as ordered. An idiosyncratic reaction is an unexpected and unusual response to a drug that is different from its known effects. A contraindication is a condition in which a drug should not be given because it could cause harm. A black box warning is the strongest warning that the Food and Drug Administration requires on a drug label, and it alerts prescribers and nurses about serious risks.

Let's talk about the heart of safe practice, the rights of medication administration. The traditional five rights are the right patient, the right drug, the right dose, the right route, and the right time. Many facilities now also include the right documentation, the right reason, and the right response, which brings us to eight rights. Let's walk through them.

The right patient means using at least two patient identifiers, such as the full name and the date of birth, before giving any medication. You should never use the room number as an identifier. Compare the identifiers with the medication administration record and the patient's wristband. If a patient is alert, ask the patient to state their name and date of birth. Do not simply say the name and wait for a yes, because a confused patient may agree to the wrong name.

The right drug means that you compare the label of the medication with the order on the medication administration record three times: when you take the drug from the storage area, before you prepare it, and at the bedside before you give it. Pay special attention to look-alike, sound-alike drug names. The right dose means that you check the calculation and the dose against the order, and you ask a second nurse to independently verify the dose for high-alert drugs. The right route means that the drug is given by the route that was ordered, and that the route is safe for the patient. For example, a patient who is vomiting or who cannot swallow may need a different route and you would clarify with the provider.

The right time means giving the drug at the scheduled time. Most facilities allow a thirty-minute window before or after the scheduled time for routine medications. Time-critical medications, such as insulin given with meals, antibiotics, and anticoagulants, should be given on time.

The right documentation means that you document the medication immediately after giving it, never before. If you document before giving, and the patient refuses, the record is wrong. The right reason means that you know why the patient is receiving the drug and that it fits the patient's condition. The right response means that you evaluate whether the drug had the intended effect and whether the patient had any adverse reaction. For example, you recheck blood pressure after giving an antihypertensive and you reassess the pain level after giving an analgesic.

The nurse also has the right to refuse to give a medication that seems wrong. If an order is unclear, incomplete, or looks unsafe, do not give the drug. Clarify with the prescriber first. If a medication error does occur, the first priority is to assess the patient and ensure safety. Then notify the provider, and complete an incident report according to the facility policy. The incident report is not part of the patient's medical record, and you should not mention it in the chart.

Medication reconciliation refers to the process of comparing the medications a patient is taking at home with the medications ordered in the hospital, in order to prevent duplications, omissions, and interactions. It should be done at admission, at transfer, and at discharge.

Now let's talk about high-alert medications. High-alert medications are drugs that carry a heightened risk of causing significant patient harm when used in error. Examples include insulin, heparin, opioids, concentrated electrolytes such as potassium chloride, and chemotherapy agents. Concentrated potassium chloride must never be given by IV push because it can cause cardiac arrest. For these drugs, use an independent double check with another licensed nurse, use smart pumps, and follow the facility protocol.

Heparin is an anticoagulant that is monitored with the activated partial thromboplastin time, or aPTT. The antidote for heparin is protamine sulfate. Warfarin is an oral anticoagulant that is monitored with the international normalized ratio, or INR. The antidote for warfarin is vitamin K. The target INR for most patients on warfarin is two to three. Teach patients taking warfarin to keep their intake of green leafy vegetables consistent because they contain vitamin K, and to report any unusual bleeding.

Digoxin is a cardiac glycoside that slows and strengthens the heartbeat. Before giving digoxin, check the apical pulse for a full minute. If the pulse is below sixty beats per minute in an adult, hold the dose and notify the provider. The therapeutic range for digoxin is zero point five to two nanograms per milliliter. Early signs of digoxin toxicity include nausea, vomiting, anorexia, and visual changes such as yellow-green halos. Low potassium increases the risk of digoxin toxicity. The antidote for digoxin toxicity is digoxin immune fab.

Acetaminophen is an analgesic and antipyretic. The main concern is liver damage in an overdose. The antidote for acetaminophen toxicity is acetylcysteine. Naloxone is the antidote for opioid overdose, and the nurse should monitor respiratory rate closely, because the effect of naloxone may wear off before the effect of the opioid. Flumazenil is the antidote for benzodiazepine overdose.

Let's talk about safe administration steps for an intramuscular injection. First, verify the order and perform hand hygiene. Second, identify the patient with two identifiers. Third, select the proper site and a needle of the correct length. Next, clean the skin with an antiseptic wipe and let it dry. Then insert the needle at a ninety-degree angle with a quick dart-like motion, aspirate only if your facility requires it, and inject the medication slowly. Finally, remove the needle, activate the safety device, dispose of the needle in a sharps container without recapping, and document the injection.

Let's finish with some priorities. Always check for allergies before giving a new drug. Check the patient's vital signs and lab values when the drug requires it. Teach the patient the name of the drug, the purpose, and the side effects to report. Never leave medication at the bedside unless there is an order and a policy that allows it. When in doubt, look it up, ask a pharmacist, or clarify with the prescriber. Thank you for watching, and remember that your best safety tool is slowing down and checking every time.
