Welcome to this lecture on fluid and electrolyte balance, with a focus on the two electrolytes that show up again and again on exams and in real patients, sodium and potassium. We will begin with how fluids move, then talk about sodium imbalances, and then spend the rest of our time on potassium, because potassium problems can stop the heart.

Let's start with the basics. The body's water is divided into two main compartments. Intracellular fluid is the fluid inside the cells, and it holds about two thirds of the body's water. Extracellular fluid is the fluid outside the cells, and it includes the fluid in the blood vessels, which is called intravascular fluid, and the fluid between the cells, which is called interstitial fluid. Sodium is the major cation in the extracellular fluid. Potassium is the major cation in the intracellular fluid. Remember that because it explains why a shift of potassium out of cells can raise the blood level so quickly.

Osmosis is defined as the movement of water across a semipermeable membrane from an area of lower solute concentration to an area of higher solute concentration. In other words, water follows sodium. Diffusion refers to the movement of solutes from an area of higher concentration to an area of lower concentration. Active transport is the movement of particles against a concentration gradient and it requires energy. The sodium-potassium pump is the classic example of active transport, moving sodium out of the cell and potassium into the cell.

Let's talk about IV fluids because they are all about tonicity. Isotonic fluids, such as normal saline and lactated Ringer's, have the same concentration as plasma, so they stay in the extracellular space and expand the intravascular volume. They are used for fluid volume deficit. Hypotonic fluids, such as half-normal saline, have a lower concentration than plasma, so water moves into the cells. They are used to treat cellular dehydration but they can cause a dangerous fluid shift, so they are avoided in patients at risk for increased intracranial pressure. Hypertonic fluids, such as three percent saline, have a higher concentration than plasma and pull water out of the cells into the vascular space. They are given only in an intensive care setting with close monitoring.

Now let's discuss sodium. The normal serum sodium level is one hundred thirty-five to one hundred forty-five milliequivalents per liter. Sodium controls water balance, helps to conduct nerve impulses, and supports muscle contraction. Hyponatremia is defined as a serum sodium level below one hundred thirty-five. The causes include vomiting, diarrhea, excessive sweating, diuretics, and syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion, or SIADH, in which the body holds on to too much water and dilutes the sodium. The signs are headache, confusion, nausea, muscle cramps, weakness, and in severe cases seizures and coma. Because the brain cells swell, neurological changes are the key warning signs. The nurse should monitor neurological status, restrict fluids if the cause is SIADH, and give sodium replacement slowly. Correcting sodium too fast can cause serious brain damage, so the correction is always gradual.

Hypernatremia is defined as a serum sodium level above one hundred forty-five. The causes include dehydration, diabetes insipidus, excess sodium intake, and inadequate water intake. The brain cells shrink, so the signs include thirst, dry mucous membranes, restlessness, agitation, confusion, and seizures. Treatment is to give fluids, usually hypotonic fluids, and to replace the water slowly. Teach the patient with hypernatremia to keep up fluid intake and avoid salty foods.

Let's turn our attention to potassium. The normal serum potassium level is three point five to five point zero milliequivalents per liter. Potassium is essential for the function of nerves, skeletal muscles, and the heart. Because potassium is so critical to the electrical activity of the heart muscle, both low and high levels can produce deadly dysrhythmias. The kidneys excrete potassium, and the body cannot store it, so patients need a daily intake of potassium.

Hypokalemia is defined as a serum potassium level below three point five. Let's talk about the causes. Potassium can be lost through the gastrointestinal tract with vomiting, diarrhea, and nasogastric suction. It can be lost through the kidneys with loop diuretics such as furosemide and thiazide diuretics. It can shift into the cells with insulin administration and with alkalosis. Poor dietary intake is another cause.

Remember what hypokalemia looks like. The muscles are weak, so you see fatigue, muscle cramps, and leg cramps. Smooth muscle slows down, causing constipation, abdominal distention, decreased bowel sounds, and paralytic ileus. Cardiac effects include weak pulses, dysrhythmias, and ECG changes such as a flattened T wave, a prominent U wave, and ST depression. Respiratory muscles can weaken, leading to shallow breathing, so assess the respiratory rate. A helpful phrase is that in hypokalemia everything is low and slow.

The treatment of hypokalemia is to replace the potassium. Oral potassium is preferred when possible. Give oral potassium with food and a full glass of water to reduce stomach irritation, and do not crush extended-release tablets. If the patient needs potassium by the IV route, there are strict safety rules. Never give potassium by IV push, because it can cause cardiac arrest. Dilute the potassium and give it slowly through an infusion pump, usually no faster than ten milliequivalents per hour on a general unit. Before giving potassium, check that the patient has adequate urine output, at least thirty milliliters per hour, because the kidneys must be able to excrete it. Use a large vein, because potassium is irritating and can cause pain and phlebitis. Place the patient on a cardiac monitor for higher infusion rates. Encourage foods high in potassium such as bananas, oranges, potatoes, tomatoes, spinach, and avocados.

Patients who take digoxin and a loop diuretic need special attention. Hypokalemia increases the risk of digoxin toxicity. So whenever you give furosemide and digoxin together, check the potassium level.

Now let's discuss hyperkalemia. Hyperkalemia is defined as a serum potassium level above five point zero. The causes include kidney failure, because the kidneys cannot excrete potassium, potassium-sparing diuretics such as spironolactone, ACE inhibitors, excessive potassium intake or supplements, tissue injury such as burns and crush injuries, and acidosis, which pushes potassium out of the cells. Blood samples that are hemolyzed can also show a falsely high potassium, so the lab may ask for a repeat.

The signs of hyperkalemia also affect nerves, muscles, and the heart. The muscles feel weak and may become paralyzed. The patient may have tingling, paresthesias, nausea, diarrhea, and abdominal cramping. The most dangerous effects are on the heart. The ECG shows tall, peaked T waves, a widened QRS complex, a prolonged PR interval, and a loss of the P wave. If it is not treated, the rhythm can progress to ventricular fibrillation or asystole. The key point to remember is that peaked T waves are the earliest ECG sign of hyperkalemia.

Let's walk through the emergency treatment of severe hyperkalemia. First, place the patient on a cardiac monitor and obtain the ECG. Second, give calcium gluconate or calcium chloride by IV, which does not lower the potassium but protects the heart from the effects of the potassium. Third, give regular insulin with dextrose, which drives potassium into the cells. Next, give a nebulized beta agonist such as albuterol, which also shifts potassium into the cells. Then use sodium bicarbonate if the patient has acidosis. Finally, remove the potassium from the body with loop diuretics, sodium polystyrene sulfonate or other binders, and in severe renal failure, dialysis. Notice that calcium, insulin, and albuterol only move or protect, but they do not remove the potassium from the body. Dialysis and the binders remove it.

The nurse should hold potassium supplements, review the patient's diet and medications, and teach the patient with kidney disease to avoid high-potassium foods and salt substitutes, because salt substitutes contain potassium chloride.

Let's quickly compare the two. In hypokalemia, the muscles are weak, the bowel is slow, and the T wave is flat. In hyperkalemia, the muscles are weak, the bowel is overactive with diarrhea, and the T wave is peaked. Always check the potassium level before you give any potassium and before you give digoxin.

Two other electrolytes are worth a brief mention because they travel with potassium problems. The normal serum calcium level is about eight point five to ten point five milligrams per deciliter, and the normal magnesium level is about one point five to two point five milligrams per deciliter. Hypocalcemia causes tingling around the mouth, muscle spasms, and a positive Chvostek and Trousseau sign, and severe cases can cause laryngospasm and seizures. Low magnesium often makes low potassium hard to correct, so the provider may replace both. Remember that calcium and magnesium are checked together with potassium whenever a patient has a dysrhythmia.

Let's finish by talking about priorities. When you see a critical potassium value, the first thing to do is assess the patient and place the patient on a cardiac monitor, then notify the provider immediately. Monitor intake and output, daily weight, and the neurological status for sodium problems. Remember that daily weight is the most accurate way to follow fluid balance, and one liter of fluid weighs about one kilogram, or two point two pounds. Thank you for watching, and good luck with your studies.
