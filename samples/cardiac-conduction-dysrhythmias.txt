Welcome to this review of the cardiac conduction system and common dysrhythmias. In this lecture we are going to connect the anatomy of the heart's electrical system to what you see on the monitor, and then to what you do at the bedside. If you understand the conduction pathway, the rhythm strips stop being random squiggles and start making sense.

Let's start with the conduction system itself. The heart has its own built-in electrical wiring. Automaticity refers to the ability of cardiac cells to generate an electrical impulse on their own without being told to by the brain. Conductivity is the ability to pass that impulse from cell to cell. Contractility is the ability of the muscle to shorten and squeeze in response to the impulse. Remember these three properties because they come up on exams.

The impulse normally begins in the sinoatrial node, also called the SA node. The SA node sits in the upper right atrium and it is the primary pacemaker of the heart. The SA node fires at an intrinsic rate of sixty to one hundred beats per minute. Because it fires fastest, it controls the heart rate and suppresses the other pacemaker sites. When the SA node is working and the rhythm originates there, we call it a sinus rhythm.

From the SA node, the impulse travels across both atria, causing atrial depolarization and atrial contraction. That electrical event appears on the electrocardiogram as the P wave. Next, the impulse arrives at the atrioventricular node, called the AV node. The AV node is located near the junction of the atria and ventricles. The AV node slows the impulse down on purpose. This delay gives the ventricles time to fill with blood before they contract. The AV node's intrinsic rate is forty to sixty beats per minute, and it serves as the backup pacemaker if the SA node fails.

After the AV node, the impulse moves down the bundle of His, then splits into the right and left bundle branches, and finally spreads through the Purkinje fibers. The Purkinje fibers carry the impulse rapidly through the ventricular muscle so that both ventricles contract together. The Purkinje fibers have an intrinsic rate of only twenty to forty beats per minute. That is the last-resort pacemaker, and a rhythm that originates here is slow and often not enough to maintain adequate cardiac output.

So the order is this. First, the SA node fires. Second, the impulse spreads across the atria. Third, the impulse is delayed at the AV node. Fourth, it travels down the bundle of His. Fifth, it moves through the bundle branches and the Purkinje fibers to the ventricles. If you can recite that sequence, you can explain almost every rhythm change we discuss today.

Now let's look at the electrocardiogram. The ECG is a graphic recording of the heart's electrical activity. It does not show how well the heart is pumping. This is a common trap. A patient can have an organized rhythm on the monitor and still have no pulse, so always assess the patient first.

The P wave represents atrial depolarization. The PR interval is measured from the start of the P wave to the start of the QRS complex, and it represents the time for the impulse to travel from the atria through the AV node. A normal PR interval is zero point twelve to zero point twenty seconds. The QRS complex represents ventricular depolarization, and a normal QRS is less than zero point twelve seconds. A wide QRS suggests the impulse is traveling abnormally through the ventricles. The T wave represents ventricular repolarization, which is the recovery phase. The QT interval is measured from the start of the QRS to the end of the T wave, and a prolonged QT interval increases the risk of a dangerous rhythm called torsades de pointes.

Repolarization is defined as the return of the cell to its resting electrical state after depolarization. Depolarization is defined as the electrical change that triggers the muscle cell to contract. If you remember that depolarization leads to contraction, you will never mix the terms up.

Let's talk about how to read a rhythm strip in a systematic way. Remember to ask the same questions every time. What is the rate? Is the rhythm regular or irregular? Is there a P wave before every QRS? Is the PR interval normal? Is the QRS narrow or wide? Using the same steps each time keeps you from missing something.

Now let's discuss the sinus rhythms. Normal sinus rhythm has a rate of sixty to one hundred, a regular rhythm, a P wave before every QRS, a normal PR interval, and a narrow QRS. Sinus bradycardia is a sinus rhythm with a rate below sixty. It can be normal in athletes and during sleep. It can also be caused by medications such as beta blockers and digoxin. The nurse should assess whether the patient is symptomatic, meaning dizzy, short of breath, hypotensive, or having chest pain. If the patient is symptomatic, atropine is the first-line drug. Sinus tachycardia is a sinus rhythm with a rate above one hundred. It is usually a response to something else such as fever, pain, anxiety, hypovolemia, or bleeding. The key idea is to treat the cause, not the number on the monitor.

Let's move on to atrial dysrhythmias. Atrial fibrillation is the most common sustained dysrhythmia. In atrial fibrillation the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, so there are no distinct P waves, and the ventricular response is irregularly irregular. Because the atria are not emptying completely, blood can pool and form clots. Clots that travel to the brain cause a stroke, so stroke prevention is a major priority. Patients with atrial fibrillation are often given anticoagulants such as warfarin, and the nurse monitors the INR. Rate control may be achieved with beta blockers, calcium channel blockers such as diltiazem, or digoxin. Atrial flutter looks different. Atrial flutter is a rapid, regular atrial rhythm that produces a sawtooth pattern on the ECG. It carries a similar stroke risk.

Supraventricular tachycardia, or SVT, refers to a fast rhythm that begins above the ventricles, typically with a rate of one hundred fifty to two hundred and a narrow QRS. For a stable patient, the first step is a vagal maneuver, such as asking the patient to bear down. If that fails, the drug of choice is adenosine. Adenosine is given as a rapid IV push followed by a saline flush, because it has an extremely short half-life. Warn the patient that they may feel brief chest pressure, flushing, or a sense of doom. Have the crash cart ready, because adenosine can cause a brief period of asystole.

Now let's talk about ventricular dysrhythmias, the ones that can kill a patient. A premature ventricular contraction, or PVC, is an early beat that originates in the ventricles. It appears wide and bizarre and has no P wave. Occasional PVCs are common and often harmless, but frequent PVCs, runs of three or more in a row, or PVCs landing on the T wave need to be reported. Check the potassium and magnesium levels, because low potassium and low magnesium make the ventricles irritable.

Ventricular tachycardia is a rapid rhythm of three or more PVCs in a row, with a wide QRS and a rate above one hundred. The most important question is whether the patient has a pulse. If the patient has a pulse and is stable, treat with an antiarrhythmic drug such as amiodarone. If the patient has a pulse but is unstable, with hypotension, chest pain, or an altered level of consciousness, the treatment is synchronized cardioversion. If the patient has no pulse, ventricular tachycardia is treated exactly like ventricular fibrillation.

Ventricular fibrillation is chaotic, disorganized electrical activity in the ventricles. The ventricles quiver and do not pump, so there is no cardiac output and no pulse. It is a medical emergency. The treatment is immediate defibrillation, along with high-quality CPR. Defibrillation refers to the delivery of an unsynchronized electrical shock to depolarize the entire heart so that the SA node can take over again. Cardioversion is different. Cardioversion is defined as a shock delivered in time with the QRS complex, which is why it is called synchronized. We use cardioversion for rhythms in which the patient has a pulse. We use defibrillation for ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia.

Let's walk through the response to an unresponsive patient in ventricular fibrillation. First, check for responsiveness and breathing and check the pulse. Second, call for help and have someone bring the defibrillator. Third, begin chest compressions right away at a rate of one hundred to one hundred twenty per minute. Next, apply the defibrillator pads and analyze the rhythm. If the rhythm is shockable, make sure everyone is clear and deliver one shock. Then immediately resume compressions for two minutes before rechecking the rhythm. Finally, give epinephrine and consider amiodarone as directed by the advanced cardiac life support protocol. Remember that compressions should be interrupted as little as possible.

Asystole is the absence of any electrical activity. The monitor shows a flat line. Before you call it, check that the leads are attached, the gain is turned up, and the patient is truly pulseless. Asystole is a nonshockable rhythm. Do not defibrillate asystole. Start CPR and give epinephrine. Pulseless electrical activity, or PEA, refers to organized electrical activity on the monitor with no palpable pulse. It is also nonshockable. We treat PEA with CPR and epinephrine while looking for the cause, such as hypovolemia, hypoxia, hydrogen ion excess meaning acidosis, hypokalemia or hyperkalemia, tension pneumothorax, tamponade, toxins, and thrombosis.

Let's finish with the heart blocks. Heart blocks are problems with conduction through the AV node. First-degree AV block is defined as a prolonged PR interval greater than zero point twenty seconds, with every impulse still conducted. It usually needs only monitoring. Second-degree AV block type one, also called Wenckebach, is a pattern in which the PR interval gets longer and longer until a QRS is dropped. It is usually benign and is monitored. Second-degree AV block type two, also called Mobitz two, has a constant PR interval with some P waves that are not followed by a QRS. This is more dangerous because it can progress suddenly to complete heart block, and the patient will likely need a pacemaker. Third-degree AV block, also called complete heart block, means that no impulses pass from the atria to the ventricles. The atria and ventricles beat independently, which is called AV dissociation. The ventricular rate is often very slow, and the patient may have syncope, hypotension, and heart failure symptoms. Treat the patient with atropine and transcutaneous pacing while preparing for a permanent pacemaker.

A pacemaker is a device that delivers an electrical impulse to stimulate the heart when the natural conduction system fails. Teach patients with a new pacemaker to check their pulse daily, avoid strong magnetic fields, carry an identification card, and report dizziness or a pulse below the set rate.

Let's pull everything together with the priorities. Always assess the patient first and the monitor second. Treat the patient, not the monitor. Airway, breathing, and circulation always come before any rhythm interpretation. Remember that the ventricular rhythms with no pulse require defibrillation, the unstable rhythms with a pulse require synchronized cardioversion, and the stable rhythms are treated with medications. Keep potassium and magnesium in the normal range, because imbalances are a leading trigger for dysrhythmias. Thank you for watching, and good luck with your review.
